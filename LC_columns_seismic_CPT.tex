\begin{abstract} In this project, seismic Cone Penetration Testing (CPT) has been evaluated as a test method to determine the properties of lime/cement (LC) columns. The reason for using seismic CPT is that there both seismic value obtained in the form of P-wave (compression wave), S-wave (shear wave) and a peak pressure. We found a connection between probing resistance of soil and strength in the stabilized columns. The results of the seismic tests obtained during fieldwork survey were evaluated and compared against seismic values obtained in the laboratory of Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI). The CPT was used to evaluate the quality of the LC columns of the reinforced soil. The adjacent projects have been based on the use of bending elements (bender elements) and resonant frequency measurement in the laboratory big project \emph{Seismic testing of stabilized soil with bender elements in triaxial tests} (Lindh, 2016a). This is a simple technique that usually gives an unequivocal answer to the resonance frequency. The homogeneity of the specimen can be examined visually and the relationship between P and S wave shows the quality of the specimen. In the field, this possibility of visually checking the quality does not exist, but here the collected measurement data must be sufficiently good. When it comes to seismic CPT, it has proven to be critical partly to evaluate whether the CPT is inside or outside the pillar. A low tip resistance may mean the probe is off the post or the post is bad. In part, one needs to be able to excite a well-defined P- and/or S-wave for the seismic measurements. Alternative seismic methods such as Up-hole technology e.t.c. should be evaluated.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Laboratory Tests}

\subsubsection{Choice of examination room}
When choosing an examination room for a test such as the one now carried out, it is desirable that the room is well examined and documented both in terms of field examinations and laboratory examinations. Furthermore, it is desirable that seismic measurements are carried out in the laboratory as a reference to the measurements in the field. In this project, the choice fell on the port of Norvik, where the area was well documented in terms of in situ conditions on natural soil and that the laboratory measurements on stabilized soil had been carried out with measurements of P-wave and flexwave (shear wave). Lindh (2016b).

\subsubsection{Determination of the material parameters of the natural clay}
The natural water ratio, wN, varies between 40 and 100\% and the yield strength, wL, also varies between 40 and 100\%. The density mostly varies between 1.5 to 1.7 tons per cubic meter. The corrected shear strength, cu,korr, is mainly between 5 and 15 kPa and the clay is low to medium sensitive.

\subsubsection{Evaluation of binders}
Testing of binding agent and amount of binding agent was carried out on clay from test excavations carried out at Norvik. To evaluate binder combinations for depth stabilization, an experimental setup called simplex centroid design was used (Montgomery, 1996, Myres and Montgomery, 1995). An experimental set-up according to the simplex centroid with three binders means that the experimental room consists of a triangle with pure binders in the corners and a mixture of two binders along the triangle's stripes. Inside the triangle is a combination of all three binders, the total binder content is constant. Each point on the triangle represents 100\% binder, but the mutual ratio between the binders varies depending on where in the triangle the test point is located. The tests were carried out according to SGI's laboratory quality handbook, document 29a, rev c. Three different binders have been used, cement, lime and lime kiln dust (English; lime kiln dust, LKD). The cement used in the tests has been a CEM II/A and the lime is a burnt lime, named Ql in the figures (from the English Quick lime).

From these three binders, nine different binder combinations have been tested. The mixing quantities corresponded to 80, 100 and 120 kg of binder per cubic meter of clay. The curing times for the samples (cement/lime 50/50) were determined to be 7, 14, 21 and 28 days. Some extra specimens were produced for printing at 80 days. 

The experimental set-up was performed as a three-factor Simplex centroid with a limitation of the LKD content. The restriction meant that the LKD content was maximized to 50\%. The trial surface in a three-factor Simplex centroid is structured as a triangle. The purpose of using this type of statistical trial planning is to minimize the number of trials while maintaining statistical significance and to ensure that both positive and negative interactions between the binder components are detected. 

Each point on the surface corresponds to 100\% binder. The selected trial points are in this case marked with blue circles, see Figure 3. Each trial point was performed as a double trial. The corners of the triangle represent 100\% of only one binder. The center of the triangle corresponds to 33\% of the three different binders. The points on the lower edge of the triangle correspond to either a pure binder, (cf. CEM II or Ql) alternatively a mixture of 50\% CEM II and 50\% Ql. Three different Simplex experiments were performed. These were equivalent to 80, 100 and 120 kg of binder per cubic meter of clay.

For this, extra reference tests were carried out which were to be used for calibration of seismic measurement in relation to compressive strength at 7 and 14 days, see Figure 4.

\subsection{Laboratory seismic measurements}
Natural resonance frequency (fn) (also called natural natural frequency) is the number of oscillations per second (Hz) in a test body that is allowed to oscillate and swing freely without damping. The lowest resonant frequency is called the fundamental mode or fundamental mode. All natural resonance frequencies can be physically related to the elastic constants, E-modulus (E) and transverse contraction number () or seismic velocities such as primary wave (P-wave) and secondary wave (S-wave or shear wave). For specimens with a length twice the diameter ($L/D\geq2$), the one-dimensional (1D) wave propagation velocity can be calculated as Equation \ref{eq:01}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:01}
	V_{P1D} =2Lf_d
\end{equation}

where $f_d$ is the damped resonance frequency (Ryd\'en, 2013).
The seismic measurements were performed as a resonance frequency measurement of P-wave and flex-wave, see Figure \ref{fig:04}.

The advantage of P-wave measurements is that these can be carried out on specimens while they harden in the sleeve without the sleeve affecting the measurements to any great extent. The disadvantage of measuring the P-wave is that it is strongly affected when the soil starts to become saturated with water, when Sr goes towards one. When the earth is saturated with water, not only the earth's P-wave velocity is measured, but also the water's P-wave velocity, which is about 1500 m/s, which means incorrect results. This is usually not a problem in laboratory-made specimens, as these cannot be saturated with water without very high pressures. 

By instead measuring the shear wave when it is not affected by high water saturation levels, this problem is bypassed. The measurement of the true shear wave is, however, more difficult with a free-free resonant column setup. A common, but not entirely accurate, way to evaluate the shear wave is to measure the bending mode also called the flex mode or transverse mode (Verástegui-Flores et al, 2015). According to this article, the shear wave velocity is underestimated by about 5\% for a specimen with a slenderness factor of 2 (=length/diameter) and a = 0.2. The setup for measuring the flex mode is shown in Figure 6.

The evaluation of the shear wave velocity is calculated according to Equation \ref{eq:02}. 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:02}
	V_s = 2Lf_f
\end{equation}

To measure true hear waves in laboratories, you have to use bending elements (so-called bender elements), this methodology is cumbersome and takes a long time. To get around this, a new methodology has been tried at SGI to evaluate the torsional mode and thus be able to better calculate the shear wave, see Figure \ref{fig:05}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_05.jpg}
	\caption{A sketch of the experimental setup for measuring torsion mode according to the free-free resonance column principle.}
\label{fig:05}
\end{figure}

However, this methodology is not yet sufficiently tested and must be verified with more measurements but also analyzed using FEM to check that the correct mode is evaluated. To evaluate how both P-wave velocity and shear strength develop with time, reference samples were manufactured. The choice of the P-wave velocity in the laboratory examinations is that it can also be measured on samples stored in sleeves. However, the length of the sleeves must be equal to the length of the sample. Otherwise, the resonance frequency of the sleeve is measured, see Appendix 1. The reference samples consisted of a standard recipe consisting of 50\% cement and 50\% quicklime. The binder quantities were chosen corresponding to 80, 100 and 120 kg/m3. After 7 days, P-wave velocity and then shear strength were evaluated on two samples from each binder quantity. This was repeated at 14, 21, 28 and 80 days. This type of correlation has previously been used for surface-stabilized specimens with good results (Rydén et al, 2006). The choice of 80 days was based on degree days to compare with the 28 day strength of samples stored at 20 oC. A better procedure would have been to use maturity numbers (MT) instead of degree days, see SGI Report 30, j.m.f. section 3.1.2.

\subsection{Seismic CPT}

The CPT probe is performed according to the standard SS-EN ISO 22476-1:2012. During a CPT probing, a cylindrical probe with a cross-sectional area of 1000 mm2 is driven where the probe has a tip angle of 60 degrees. The lowering takes place at a constant speed of 20 mm/s. At the probe tip, the force required to drive the probe down is measured. Through a backlash coupling, the casing friction is measured, which can thus be distinguished from the tip pressure. Furthermore, the pore pressure generated during the drive-down is measured via a filter system just behind the probe tip. Figure \ref{fig:06} shows the construction of a CPT probe.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_06.jpg}
	\caption{Schematic diagram of the composition of a CPT probe. From SGI Information 15.}
\label{fig:06}
\end{figure}

During the probing, the tip resistance is recorded, which is the force per unit area obtained by dividing the measured force by the cross-sectional area of the tip. This peak pressure is denoted qc if the pore pressure is not taken into account and qt if the value is corrected taking into account error sources from the pore effect. In the special case when $u \approx 0$ or is negligible, qc becomes qt. The tip resistance is given in MPa or kPa. The CPT probe can be reported according to Figure \ref{fig:07}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_07.jpg}
	\caption{A protocol with standard scales evaluated using Conrad software, version 3.1.}
\label{fig:07}
\end{figure}

Seismic CPT involves, in addition to a regular CPT sounding, also a measurement of shear and compression waves. The measurements are performed with a series-connected device that is placed between the CPT probe and the probing rods. The measurements are usually performed by stopping the CPT probe at the level you want to measure at and the measurement is performed. However, there are systems with continuous measurement, but this must be stopped after all when splicing bars.

There are two different principles, one principle is based on two rounds of accelerometers with a fixed distance between the accelerometers, Figure \ref{fig:08} (a). The accelerometers measure in the x-, y- and z-direction, where y-led is into the pitch of the character. This method is sometimes referred to as “true-time”, as the fixed distance between the accelerometers provides a safer determination of the time difference and thus a better determination of the ground running speed, Figure \ref{fig:08} (a). Here the accelerometers are described with A1 and A2.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\begin{subfigure}[b]{0.7\textwidth}
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Fig_08a.jpg}
		\caption{CPT probe with dual accelerometers.}
	\end{subfigure} \\ \vfill
	\begin{subfigure}[b]{0.7\textwidth}
		\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Fig_08b.jpg}
		\caption{CPT probe with one accelerometer.}
	\end{subfigure}
	\caption{Schematic diagram of a seismic CPT probe}
\label{fig:04}
\end{figure}

This principle is called "true-time" as the calculation of walking speed is based on the time measurement between the first wave propagation to accelerometers 1 and 2. As the distance between the accelerometers is constant, the speed can be calculated directly.
The second principle is based on carrying out the measurements with only one set of accelerometers with registration in three directions (x-, y- and z-direction). The measurement is first performed at one level, after which the CPT probing continues and a new measurement is performed at the next level, Figure \ref{fig:08} (b). The accelerometer measures in the x-, y- and z-direction, where y-led is into the pitch of the character. This method is sometimes called Pseudo-time, since the distance between the different measurements is based on two different measurements where the difference in depth is taken from the CPT logging. Here, a depth measurement d1 is first performed and the probe is pressed down to depth d2 where the next measurement is performed, see Figure \ref{fig:08} (b).

\subsubsection{Evaluation of CPT}

The evaluation of the CPT probe has been performed using Conrad version 3.1. In a fine-grained soil, the shear strength is primarily evaluated as the net peak pressure. An alternative method for clays is to use the generated pore overpressure. The empirical relationships developed for Swedish conditions are based on a wealth of field and laboratory data from a variety of soil types. In a natural clay soil, the relationship between net peak pressure and shear strength is sensitive to the soil's yield strength wL and to some extent also to the degree of over-consolidation OCR, which is often described according to the relationship in Equation \ref{eq:03}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:03}
	c_u = \frac{q_t -\sigma _{\nu 0}}{13,4+6,65W_L} {\left(\frac{OCR}{1,3}\right)}^{-0,2}
\end{equation}

In cases where a measure of the clay's yield strength is missing, the relationship of the Equation \ref{eq:04} is used instead. 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:04}
	c_u \approx \frac{q_t -\sigma _{\nu 0}}{N_{kt}}
\end{equation}

Where $N_{kt}$ (empirical confactor) is usually set to 16.3 for a clay. In the evaluation program Conrad, $N_{kt}$ is denoted by $N_{11}$. For a binder-stabilized soil, there is not the same amount of empirical evidence and therefore not much literature about which value of $N_{kt}$ should be used. There are certain recommendations that $N_{kt}$ should be set somewhere between 17 and 20 for a stabilized soil (Makusa, 2013; Larsson, 2017).

Furthermore, there are limitations with evaluating the firmness in different layers of highly layered soil as the tip pressure is affected by the above as underlying layer at a distance of 5 to 20 tip diameters. In order for the method to be able to evaluate a firmer layer in a loose soil, the thickness of the layer must be 4.4 to 0.7 meters. In the reverse situation with a looser layer in a firmer soil, the thickness of the dirt should be 0.2 to 0.4 meters. This means that zones of weakness in a LC pillar can easily be missed in a CPT evaluation. This becomes most important for the evaluation of a LC column reinforcement in the shear zone.

In a binder-stabilized soil, pozzolanic reactions take place for many months, which is why the properties of a stabilized soil partly change over time, but also undergo a change in terms of texture, etc. The change in the clay's texture is based on ion exchange in the clay and on a change in the water ratio. The ion exchange is described by the lyotropic series, as below (Andersson, 1960): 

\ch{Li+} $<$ \ch{Na+} $<$ \ch{H+} $<$ \ch{K+} $<$ \ch{NH4+} $<$$<$ \ce{Mg^{++}} $<$ \ch{Ca^{++}} $<$$<$ \ch{Al^{+++}}.

The change in the structure of the clay mineral is shown in Figure \ref{fig:09}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_09.jpg}
	\caption{Change in texture and water-holding of clay as it moves from a sodium saturated system to a calcium-saturated system. Modified after Little (1997).}
\label{fig:09}
\end{figure}

This means that the stabilized clay has a change in plasticity, yield strength, etc. The degree of change depends both on the type of clay mineral but also on the type of binder. These changes to the structure of the stabilized clay mean that the empirical relationships that exist between tip pressure and the shear strength of the clay are not correct for a stabilized clay. 

To be able to evaluate samples stored at different temperatures, e.g. samples stored in climate rooms at 7 oC and samples that are manufactured in situ with a different temperature development and cooling compared to laboratory stored samples can be used. The maturity number is defined according to Equation \ref{eq:05}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:05}
	M_T =[ 20+(T-20)\times K]^2 \times \sqrt{t}
\end{equation}

Where T = temperature (\textdegree C), t = time (days), K = material-dependent empirical constant.
The material-dependent empirical constant K is the most easily calculated by storing samples at different temperatures, e.g., 7 and 20\textdegree C. The samples are then measured with resonance frequency measurement at different time intervals. The results from the seismic measurements are then used to fit the curves so that they coincide, i.e. different values of K are used, after which the curves are compared. A common value of K is 0.5, but the literature has also shown $K \approx 1$ (Åhnberg and Holm, 1987).

\subsection{Results of the fieldwork}

The sounding itself was carried out like a traditional CPT sounding, with the difference that at the depth where the seismic measurements were to be carried out, the CPT sounding itself was paused and the P- and S-wave measurements began. The measurements were started by using a sledgehammer which was connected with an earth cable to the measuring equipment to generate shear and compression waves respectively. In the beginning, problems arose with the signal, which could be traced, among other things, to motor vibrations and transmission between drill chuck and drill steel. This was resolved by releasing the drill chuck and shutting down the motor of the drill track carriage during the seismic measurements. 

The CPT soundings were never performed at the center of the columns to avoid disturbances at the center of the columns arising from the column installation itself. The results from the CPT soundings are also reported in Figures XXXX.

In total, 7 different KC pillars with a varying age between 9 days and 49 days were examined. The data for the investigated pillars can be found in Table 1.

As there are significant uncertainties surrounding the evaluation of a stabilized soil's shear strength based on peak pressure or pore pressure build-up from CPT sounding, this report has focused on comparing standardized values between peak pressure, qc and standardized results from seismic measurements. The normalization was carried out by dividing the results by the largest value. The seismic measurements were recorded in three lines, of which x- and y-lines represent shear wave measurements and z-lines represent compression wave measurements. The evaluations are reported in Appendix 3.  

For KC column acs155, measured parameters are reported in Figure 24.

The result shows a large variation, more very low tip resistance between 2 and 2.5 m below the ground surface, after which the tip pressure increases up to approx. 10 MPa at the level of 3.5 meters. Between 5 and 8 m, the peak pressure is between 1 and 3 MPa and at 8 meters rises steeply to over 10 MPa. The sounding was interrupted at the depth of 10 m due to stoppage due to high tip pressure. Measured friction shows consistent results but with some delay at the end of the probe. The results from the seismic measurements show good agreement between shear waves in the x and y directions. However, the high peak pressure around the level of 3.5 m u.my is not captured. The compression wave shows a better agreement with the peak pressure and the evaluated P-wave velocities, see Figure 25. The P-wave velocity down to the level of 7 m is within the compression wave velocities measured in the laboratory, see Figure 26 and Figure 21. The values are, however, quite high, especially considering that the age of the pillar at the time of probing was only 26 days.

The measured shear wave speed for the column is reported in Figure 27.

The graph shows results from evaluated shear wave excited from both right and left in x-direction. The results show low shear wave velocities. Table 2 shows P- and S-wave velocities for column acs155. The table also contains calculated shear strength values based on laboratory determined relationships.

It is clear that the evaluated strengths from the P-wave measurement are relatively high and probably above the actual strength, while the strengths evaluated from the S-wave measurements are clearly below the expected strengths. Down to a depth of 6 meters, the evaluated strengths are only slightly above the initial strengths of the natural clay. Figure 24 shows the probing result from pillar acs155. This result indicates a significant difference compared to a probing in natural clay, cf. Figure 38. 

The results from the probing of pillar acs155 indicate that it is not possible to evaluate any reliable results based on the seismic CPT. The CPT sounding itself shows that the soil has been reinforced with a very solid part around 3.5 m below the ground surface.

The evaluated CPT sounding for pillar ACW151 is reported in Figure 28. The peak pressure varies from below 0.5 MPa to above 4 MPa. This may appear to be low for a pillar that was 28 days old at the time of probing.

For column acw151, the standardized values between peak pressure, P- and S-wave show poor agreement, see Figure 29. The evaluated S-wave gives a fairly good agreement between x- and y-direction, but does not follow the results from the peak pressure. The highest evaluated P-wave speed is almost 5000 m/s, which is in parity with the speed in steel.

In Figure 30, the probing results from the CPT probing of pillar ada147 are reported. The tip pressure varies from about 0.5 MPa up to over 6 MPa. This shows a very large variation in strength and indicates difficulties in assessing the strength of a column based on CPT probing.

Figure 31 shows standardized values for column ada147. The shear wave velocities, see appendix 3, show low strengths. The seismic determinations give a bulk modulus which does not capture the large variations in peak pressure but rather gives an average value. The correlation between peak pressure and shear wave velocities is low. The evaluated compression wave speed shows values above 2000 m/s, which is not credible.

Column adc148 also shows large variations in tip pressure. The CPT probe appears to have exited the pillar about 9 meters below the ground surface, see Figure 32.

This column shows an abnormally large difference in shear wave between x-direction and y-direction. Neither the S- nor P-wave velocities show good agreement with the tip resistance, see Figure 33. The P-wave velocities vary between 1000 and 2000 m/s which does not reflect the expected strength.

The sounding result from pillar aea358 shows a similar pattern to the other pillars, see Figure 34. The peak pressure shows very low values at 7.5 meters u.my.

The evaluated shear wave velocities give a credible shear strength, c.m.f. Appendix 3, but does not reflect the variation in peak pressure, see Figure 35.

Figure 36 shows probing results for column aeg35. This sounding also shows large variations in tip pressure, which makes it difficult to find a connection between the CPT sounding results and the shear wave velocities, see Figure 37.

\section{Conclusion}

The laboratory testing showed a high repeatability between trials. The spread in compressive strength/shear strength increased slightly with increased strength, which is expected as minor inaccuracies in the test specimens have a greater effect on strength. 

The requirements for the K/C columns in this project were a minimum in situ shear strength of 150 kPa after 28 days. To ensure this, the requirement for the laboratory packed samples is set to at least 200 kPa. Several of the mixtures that contained both cement, quicklime and LKD met the requirements of at least 200 kPa after 28 days of storage. Since the temperature in situ in the columns is partly dependent on the type of binder and the amount of binder, it is difficult to translate laboratory results with field results, especially when the laboratory-wrapped samples were stored at 7 oC in a climate room where the cooling is significantly greater than in an in situ condition. 

The results from the field test show large variations in tip resistance, but also large variations in shear and compression wave velocities. These results were partly expected but the hypothesis was to find the same trends in peak pressure and in seismic results.

At the seismic CPT there are several uncertainty factors:

\begin{itemize}
	\item How are the generated S and P waves transmitted to the pillar? 
	\item Is there "crosstalk" from other sources of vibration?
	\item Is the probe in or coming out of the column?
	\item How well shear and compression waves are generated at great depths? 
\end{itemize}
    
The seismic CPT has many advantages over traditional CPT. To ensure that the CPT probe is in the pillar, a resistivity CPT can be connected there as the stabilized soil has a much lower resistivity due to free ions (Lindh et al, 2000). One of the great advantages of seismic CPT is that one obtains both a measure of the compressive force and a determination of P- and S-waves. A not entirely unimportant parameter for seismic CPT is cost. Here, both a drilling rig and special equipment are required, as well as the fact that the method is currently quite time-consuming. By using a better signal source, some of the disadvantages of the method can be eliminated. The methodology can be a way to evaluate KC columns but cannot compete in terms of cost with the limestone column probe.

An alternative to the above is to combine the limestone probe with seismic CPT. In cases where a more reliable determination of the properties is needed, the author would recommend that the below alternatives be tried. 

An alternative to using seismic CPT is the so-called "up hole" technique for the quality control of the KC columns. The methodology involves installing a pipe in or near the center of the pillar. The technology that was developed for the installation of measuring pipes in connection with the investigations for SD Work Report 35, which means that the pipe is installed with the help of the KC pillar machine. This can be a good solution to quickly and easily install the pipes. 

After an initial hardening, the measurements can begin. On the top of the KC pillar, for example, four accelerometers are installed, positioned with a 90 degree angle between the accelerometers. The measurement consists of a P- and S-wave source being lowered into the pipe to the desired depth, after which the probe is clamped as a "packer" and the waves are excited and the walking speed is measured, Figure 39. The experiment is repeated in the directions where the accelerometers are placed. After the measurements on one level, the probe is moved up to the next level, after which the measurements continue.

The advantage of this method is that you can ensure a good contact between pillars and accelerometers and that the excitation wave and the location of the source are very well defined. An additional advantage is that the measurements can be carried out by one person without costs for drilling rigs, etc. 

The disadvantages that have been identified are that the quality of the pillar is usually the worst at the top of the pillar. This can be solved either by excavating the top or by attaching the accelerometers at a deeper level. A disadvantage compared to seismic CPT is that you have to decide in advance which columns are to be measured.

In order to obtain a direct comparison with the limestone probe, a slotted pipe can be used for the "up hole" measurements, after which a normal probing with the limestone probe is carried out.

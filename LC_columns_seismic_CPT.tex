\begin{abstract} In this project, seismic Cone Penetration Testing (CPT) has been evaluated as a test method to identify subsurface conditions for determination of the properties of clayey soil stabilized with lime/cement (LC) columns in the region of Stockholm Norvik Port, Sweden. Using applied geophysical methods, we found a connection between the probing resistance of soil and strength in the stabilized columns. The results of the seismic tests obtained during fieldwork survey were evaluated and compared against seismic values obtained in the laboratory of Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI) through measurements of P- and S-wave velocities of specimens. The CPT profiles were used to evaluate the quality of the LC columns of the reinforced soil by interpretation of the recorded signals. The adjacent projects were based on the use of bending elements and resonant frequency measurement in the laboratory project of SGI using technique of evaluating resonance frequency of the stabilized soil specimens. The homogeneity of clay samples was examined visually and the relationship between the P and S waves demonstrated the gain in strength during stabilization of the specimens over time. Applied geophysical methods enable to evaluate the quality of soil to achieve sufficient quality of foundations prior to construction of infrastructure objects. Seismic CPT has proven to be an effective method essential to evaluate whether the CPT is inside or outside the column. A low tip resistance indicate that the probe is off the column placement or the stabilization is not efficient yet. This work demonstrated the effectiveness of the alternative seismic methods supporting up-hole technology of drilling techniques for practical purpose in civil geotechincal works and engineering geology.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

Stabilization of soil is a fundamental issue in civil engineering. Prior to construction works, weak soils should be stabilized using binders such as cement \cite{Bache,Chenetal2021,LindhLemenkova2022b}, lime \cite{Kichouetal,Chand}, slag \cite{LindhLemenkova2021a} or others \cite{Mirzababaei}. Compared to native soil, stabilized mixture exhibits increased improved properties such as increased strength, enhanced elastic modulus and stiffness \cite{Madhyannapuetal2010,SUNDARY2022103299}, improved liquefaction resistance \cite{ITO199433}, reduced porosity, permeability and shrinkage \cite{TONINIDEARAUJO2023129757,Nagih,Ahnberg2003}, decreased swell potential in soils prone to freeze-thaw cycles \cite{ShihataBaghdadi,BinShafique,Orakoglu}, increased resistance against the impacts from moisture and temperature \cite{Zhangetal2020,Kimetal2012}. Such improved properties make soil suitable to earthworks and ensure the safety of constructions and civil infrastructure systems (roads, communications, tunnels, highways). This especially concerns the high plasticity expansive clay soils of Sweden. Existing methods of soil stabilization can be divided into two broad categories: traditional methods of soil stabilization and evaluation of strength \cite{Trhl,LindhLemenkova2022a,Heidarizadeh} and advanced seismic methods using non-destructive techniques of acoustical soundings \cite{Varma,Foti,LindhLemenkova2021b,GarciaSuarez}. The traditional methods of evaluation of stabilized soil improvements using uniaxial \cite{Avci,Lapointe,XuYi} or triaxial \cite{ALVARADO} tests and laboratory equipment are robust and widely applied approaches of soil evaluation. However, they have certain limitations and disadvantages. First, these methods are destructive, i.e., the tested specimens are crashed after the experiments. Besides, these tests are not applicable to conduct on fissured clay. Moreover, the Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS) may be not precise for soils with the angle of shearing resistance not equal to zero where shear strength is not equal to half the compressive strength. On that basis, the use of the applied methods of data analysis based on mathematical modelling \cite{Jefferies} and image analysis \cite{Kallenetal2014,Kallenetal2016} is applicable for evaluation of soil characteristics.

The applied geophysical methods have been widely used in the context of seismic and acoustical tests. These are based on the evaluation of the velocities of P and S waves penetrating the soil \cite{Safaee,LindhLemenkova2022c,FotiLancellotta}. Other examples include Rayleigh-Ritz method which evaluates the free vibration characteristics in the ground \cite{SonkarMittal,Jones} or attenuation of waves through the evaluation of vibration over time \cite{Colombero}. Several experimental papers were published on application of wave velocity for evaluation soil parameters. Among these, \cite{Santamarina} reports on the existing relationship between wave propagation and the inherent properties of the materials, such as fabric, mineralogical structure, surface roughness, size of particles and angularity, which control soil strength and stiffness.

Ultrasonic and bender element tests used to measure elastic modulus and evaluation the rigidity of soil as shear modulus from compression waves are reported in \cite{Amaral} with a case of sand samples stabilized by cement. The use of free-free resonant column tests in combination with UCS tests has also proved to be applicable to the sand-cement mixtures for measurement of the electrical resistivity and mechanical properties of stabilized soil \cite{Rusati}. Other examples in this category include measuring shear wave velocity during penetration testing \cite{Hepton1989}, and determining the dynamic shear modulus of the ground, independent of Poisson's ratio \cite{Abbiss}. 

Cone Penetration Testing (CPT) is an effective method of soil investigation and identification subsurface conditions. Originally developed in the Netherlands in the 1930s and initially named as the Dutch cone test \cite{brouwer2007situ,mccallum_2014}, it has nowadays numerous applications in geotechnical engineering, such as prediction of liquefaction resistance \cite{Olsen,Fitzgerald,Saye}, evaluation of strength \cite{Priceetal2016,Jamiolkowski}, and measuring excess pore pressure \cite{SullyCampanella,Elsworthetal2006}. Nowadays, CPT is one of the most used methods for measurements of geotechnical properties of soil. For instance, the applications of CPT for estimation of deformation modulus of soil by \cite{SHAHIEN2013633,BENZNAVARRETE2022289} and characterizing soil liquefaction potential by \cite{GUAN20221221} are worth mentioning. related methods include estimating cone penetration resistance for analysis of soil compressibility \cite{Bishtetal2021}, 

The deep soil mixing (DSM) method is an \emph{in situ} stabilization of soil in which soil is mixed with binders, typically cementitious materials, lime, slag or similar stabilizing agents. Over the last decades, there has been a rise in research into DSM methods, as a response to the appearance of novel construction practices and equipment in addition to existing general techniques of drilling in cored boreholes \cite{Hepton}. These are the techniques of stabilizing unsaturated expansive subsoils of moderate active depths \cite{MadhyannapuPuppala,Madhyannapu}, settlement control in highway embankment \cite{Archeewa}. Cases of the reinforced embankments supported by DSM cement columns are presented by \cite{Lambrechts} for stability and the settlement control, \cite{Bergado}
with computed permeability and compressibility ratio and the consolidation of the columns and clay, and \cite{Yapageetal2014} with computed settlements, excess pore-water pressures and lateral deformations in strain-softening behavior of deep cement columns.

Cohesive soil with high moisture content and fine-grained structure, such as clays, silts or loams, are best stabilized by lime and cementitious binders \cite{Lindh2004}. As a result, soil has enhanced parameters which result in the increased strength, reduced permeability and compressibility. However, the engineering properties of soil stabilized by the DSM method depend on various factors including the following: the original characteristics of the native soil, types and amount of binders, technical and operational parameters defined by construction types, curing time, depth, external parameters such as temperature and moisture and loading conditions.

In this paper, we propose a framework of novel applications of DSM and seismic methods to address the problem of evaluation of strength of the expansive clay stabilized with lime/cement (LC) columns. 

The methodology includes the \emph{in-situ} fieldwork and laboratory based measurements and modelling. The CPT soundings were performed in the area of Stockholm Norvik Port. The resistance and strength of soil in the stabilized columns was evaluated by seismic tests. A method of resonant frequency measurements of compressional wave velocities was embedded into these tests. We applied the alternative seismic methods supporting up-hole technology of drilling techniques. This enabled to evaluate the pressure by S- and P-wave for LC columns. We report the results of 7 columns with performed CPT probing (ACS155, ACW151, ADA147, ADC148, AEG35, AEE356 and AEA358). We evaluated the relationship between the P- and S-waves which shown gain in strength.

\subsection{Objectives and goals}
The background to the project is the need to adequately evaluate the quality of lime-cement (LC) columns in terms of strength and homogeneity in the region of Stockholm Norvik Port. Norvik consisted of a bay until the beginning of the 1980s, surrounded by a hilly strip of land and a mountainous island. Nowadays, this area is largely reconstructed for container terminal of Stockholm Norvik Port, a new deep sea port of Sweden in the Baltic Sea. The Stockholm Norvik Port is an important port transporting cargo in the capital with a direct connection to other regions of Sweden in the Baltic Sea and northern Europe. The sustainable and efficient operation of the infrastructure in the port modalities requires safe constructions on the stabilized ground. Therefore, the objectives of this work are to determine the strength properties of soil stabilized by lime-cement (LC) columns using Cone Penetration Testing (CPT). The CPT was evaluated as a test method for the stabilization of soil prior to construction works. The practical goal of this work is to connect the laboratory measurements and field measurements to obtain a better and safer optimization of binders and binder types for soil stabilization. In the response to these needs, the aim is to evaluate seismic methods for evaluating the strength of the soil, stabilized by various binders.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Fieldwork}
In this project, the field investigations were carried out during the period of 7--10 November 2016 in Norvik. The technical equipment included a multipurpose drilling rig GEORIG -- Model 607 used for soundings. The GEORIG -- 607, developed by Geotech AG, was selected as a proper instrument for drilling, since it is equipped with the necessary devices for soil rock drilling, Swedish weight sounding, dynamic sounding, Standard Penetration Test (SPT), Cone Penetration Testing (CPT). The GEORIG 607 was anchored with a screw to achieve the sufficient holding force, Figure \ref{fig:01}.

During the construction of a gas storage facility in Nyn\"ashamn, the bay was filled with the explosives from the construction. The filling was carried out by the tipping masses from several fronts, which meant that the large volumes of clay were enclosed in the blast-stone filling. The two large areas, northern and southern ones, were identified and used as study areas, filled with up to 30 m of clay. Previously, a number of geotechnical field investigations in this area was carried out in 1982, 2008, 2010 and 2011 with discussed technical details regarding clay properties and stabilization \cite{Geomind2015}.

The requirements for the LC columns in this project were set on achieving a minimum \emph{in situ} shear strength of 150 kPa after 28 days of curing period. To ensure this, the requirement for the laboratory packed samples is set to at least 200 kPa. Several mixtures of the stabilized soil that contained cement, quicklime and LKD met the requirements of at least 200 kPa after 28 days of storage. Since the temperature \emph{in situ} in the columns is partly dependent on the type and the amount of binder, the laboratory tests varied from the field results, especially when the laboratory cured samples were stored at 7\textdegree C in a climate room where the cooling is significantly greater than in an \emph{in situ} conditions. 

\subsection{Laboratory Tests}

\subsubsection{Test chamber}

Prior to tests, the test chamber was well examined and documented both in terms of fieldwork and laboratory examinations. Furthermore, seismic measurements were carried out in the laboratory as a reference to the measurements in the field. In this project, the study area included the Stockholm Norvik port, which is well documented in terms of the \emph{in situ} conditions on natural soil. The laboratory measurements on stabilized soil were carried out using seismic tests of P-wave and flexwave (shear wave) on the stabilized samples \cite{Lindh2016b}.

\subsubsection{Determination of the material parameters}
The soil stabilized in this project included natural clay with material parameters reported in Figure \ref{fig:02} and Figure \ref{fig:03}. The overall density values vary in the range of $1,50 to 2,00 t/m^3$ in the depth up to 25 m. The water ration ($W_N$) of soil varies from 40 to 100. The natural water ratio, wN, varies between 40 and 100\% and the yield strength, wL, also varies between 40 and 100\%. The density mostly varies between 1.5 to 1.7 tons per $m^3$. The corrected shear strength, $c_u , korr$, is mainly between 5 and 15 kPa and the clay is low to medium, in terms of sensitivity. 

\subsubsection{Binders}

Three different binders were used in this study: 1) ordinary Portland cement (OPC) of type CEM II/A; 2) burnt lime, or quick lime (Ql); 3) lime kiln dust (LKD). These binders were selected due to their effectiveness and applicability, as tested in previous studies \cite{LindhLemenkova2022CEE}. From the three binders, nine different binder combinations have been tested. The mixing quantities corresponded to 80, 100 and 120 kg of binder per $m^3$ of clay. 
To evaluate binder combinations for stabilization using deep mixing method, an experimental setup using simplex centroid design was used following the existing methodology \cite{electronics11223726}. Hence, various percentage of the three binders was tested in an experiment which consists of a triangle with pure binders in the corners and a mixture of two binders along the triangle's stripes. 

A combination of all the three binders was tested experimentally inside the triangle with a total binder content as a constant. The trial surface in a three-factor simplex centroid is structured as a triangle with the corners of the triangle representing 100\% of one binder and the center of the triangle corresponds to 33\% of the three different binders. The mutual ratio between the binders varies depending on where in the triangle the test point is located. The tests were carried out according to the laboratory quality handbook of the Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI), document 29a, rev c. Each trial point was performed as a double trial. The points on the lower edge of the triangle correspond to either a pure binder, (cf. CEM II or Ql) alternatively a mixture of 50\% CEM II and 50\% Ql. Testing the proportions and the amount of binders was carried out on clay collected from the test excavation sites carried out at Norvik. 

The curing times for the samples was determined as 7, 14, 21 and 28 days with measurements of strength performed on the reference days. Some extra specimens were evaluated on the 80th day of curing. Extra reference tests were carried out which were to be used for calibration of seismic measurement in relation to compressive strength at 7 and 14 days.The experimental setup was performed as a three-factor simplex centroid with a restriction limitation of the LKD content which was maximized to 50\%. The purpose of the statistical trial planning is to minimize the number of trials in view of the large amount of materials that should be tested in real conditions (dozens tons of soil) and to optimize the binder blends \cite{Lindh2001}. At the same time, the experimental planning maintains the statistical significance and ensures that both positive and negative interactions between the binder components are detected. 

\subsection{Seismic tests}

Natural resonance frequency (fn) (natural frequency) is the number of oscillations per second (Hz) in a test body that is allowed to oscillate and swing freely without damping. The lowest resonant frequency is called the fundamental mode or fundamental mode. All natural resonance frequencies can be physically related to the elastic constants, E-modulus (E) and transverse contraction number or seismic velocities such as primary wave (P-wave) and secondary wave (S-wave or shear wave). For specimens with a length twice the diameter ($L/D\geq2$), the one-dimensional (1D) wave propagation velocity was calculated using Equation \ref{eq:01}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:01}
	V_{P1D} =2Lf_d
\end{equation}

where $f_d$ is a damped resonance frequency \cite{Rydenetal2006}.
Seismic measurements were performed as a resonance frequency measurement of P-wave and S-wave, Figure \ref{fig:04}.

The advantage of the P-wave measurements is that these can be carried out on the specimens while they harden in the sleeve without the sleeve affecting the measurements to any great extent. The disadvantage of measuring the P-wave is that it is strongly affected when soil starts to become saturated with water, when Sr goes towards one. When soil is saturated with water, not only the P-wave velocity of soil sample is measured, but also the P-wave velocity of water, which is about 1500 m/s. This may lead to the incorrect results. However, this bias is not significant in the laboratory-made specimens, because these cannot be saturated with water without very high pressures. 

Measuring the shear wave instead enables to solve problem in cases when it is not affected by high water saturation levels. However, the measurement of the true shear wave is more difficult with a free-free resonant column setup. A common, way to evaluate shear wave, although not entirely accurate, is to measure the bending mode, i.e., flex mode or transverse mode \cite{VERASTEGUIFLORES2015943}. According to this approach, shear wave velocity is underestimated by ca. 5\% for a specimen with a slenderness factor of 2 (slenderness is a ratio of length/diameter) and a = 0.2. The setup for measuring flex mode is shown in Figure \ref{fig:04} (b).

The evaluation of the shear wave velocity is calculated according to Equation \ref{eq:02}. 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:02}
	V_s = 2Lf_f
\end{equation}

To measure true hear waves in laboratories, you have to use bending elements (so-called bender elements), this methodology is cumbersome and takes a long time. To get around this, a new methodology has been tried at SGI to evaluate the torsional mode and thus be able to better calculate shear wave, Figure \ref{fig:05}. However, this methodology is not yet sufficiently tested and must be verified with more measurements and analysis using FEM to check that the correct mode is evaluated. 

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_05.jpg}
	\caption{A sketch of the experimental setup for measuring torsion mode according to the free-free resonance column principle.}
\label{fig:05}
\end{figure}

To evaluate how both P-wave velocity and shear strength develop with time, reference samples were fabricated. The choice of the P-wave velocity in the laboratory tests is that it can also be measured on samples stored in sleeves. However, the length of the sleeves must be equal to the length of the sample. Otherwise, the resonance frequency of the sleeve is measured which biases the results. The reference samples consisted of a standard recipe consisting of 50\% OPC and 50\% QL. 

The binder quantities were chosen corresponding to 80, 100 and 120 $kg/m^3$. After 7 days of curing, the P-wave velocity and shear strength were evaluated on the two samples from each binder quantity. This was repeated on days 14, 21, 28 and 80, respectively. This type of correlation has previously been used for the surface-stabilized specimens and indicated robust results \cite{ma15217798}. The choice of 80 days was based on degree days to compare with the 28 day strength of samples stored at 20\textdegree C. A better procedure would have been to use maturity numbers (MT) instead of the degree days.

\subsection{Seismic CPT}

The Cone Penetration Testing (CPT) probe is performed according to the standard SS-EN ISO 22476-1:2012 for subsurface exploration. The reason for using seismic CPT is that there both seismic value obtained in the form of P-wave (compression wave), S-wave (shear wave) and a peak pressure. 

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_06.jpg}
	\caption{Schematic diagram of the composition of a CPT probe. Modified after: SGI Information 15.}
\label{fig:06}
\end{figure}

During the CPT probing, a cylindrical probe with a cross-sectional area of $1000 mm^2$ is driven, where the probe has a tip angle of 60 \textdegree. The pushing of the instrumented cone into the ground was performed at a continuous rate of $2,0 cm/s$. During the CPT, the force required to drive the probe down was measured by mechanical measurements to evaluate the penetration resistance of soil to pushing a cone with a conical tip into the soil. The casing friction was measured through a backlash coupling, to distinguish it from the tip pressure. The pore pressure generated during the pushing was measured using a filter system located behind the probe tip, and the friction force gauge was used to measure the friction ratio between sleeve friction and tip resistance, which measured as a percentage, see the scheme in Figure \ref{fig:06}.

During probing, the tip resistance (MPa) is recorded as a force required to push the tip of the cone through soil. It is determined as the force per unit area obtained by dividing the measured force by the cross-sectional area of the tip. This peak pressure is denoted as \emph{qc} if the pore pressure is not taken into account and \emph{qt} if the value is corrected taking into account error sources from the effects of the soil pores. In special case, when $u \approx 0$ or is negligible, \emph{qc} becomes \emph{qt}. The CPT probe is reported and registered in standard protocols using Conrad software, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:07}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_07.jpg}
	\caption{A protocol with standard scales evaluated using Conrad software, version 3.1.}

The sounding was carried out following the traditional methods of the CPT sounding. The specifics of our fieldwork is that at the depth where seismic measurements were carried out, the CPT sounding was paused, while the P- and S-wave measurements started.

The measurements started by using a sledgehammer which was connected with an earth cable to the measuring equipment to generate shear and compression waves, respectively. In the beginning, problems arose with the signal, which could be traced, among other things, to motor vibrations and transmission between the drill chuck and drill steel. This was resolved by releasing the drill chuck and shutting down the motor of the drill track carriage during the seismic measurements. The CPT soundings were not performed at the centre of the columns, to avoid disturbances at the centre of the columns arising from the column installation itself. 

Seismic CPT included measurement of shear and compression waves, in addition to the regular CPT sounding. The measurements were performed using a series-connected device that was placed between the CPT probe and the probing rods. The measurements were performed by stopping the CPT probe at the required level of 25 meters. In other cases, the systems with continuous measurement can be used, which should be be stopped after splicing the bars.

There are two different principles of measurements.

The first principle is based on the two rounds of accelerometers (A1 and A2) with a fixed distance between them, Figure \ref{fig:08} (a). The accelerometers measure the x-, y- and z-directions, where y-direction identifies the pitch. This principle calculates the penetration speed based on the time measurement between the first wave propagation to accelerometers 1 and 2. Therefore, this method is sometimes referred to as “true-time”, since the fixed distance between the accelerometers provides a safer determination of the time difference and thus a precise determination of the ground running speed, Figure \ref{fig:08} (a). 
As the distance between the accelerometers is constant, the speed can be calculated directly.

The second principle is based on carrying out the measurements with only one set of accelerometers with registration in three directions (x, y and z). The measurement is first performed at one level, after which the CPT probing continues and a new measurement is performed at the next level, Figure \ref{fig:08} (b). In this case, the accelerometer measures in the x-, y- and z-direction, where y-direction identifies the pitch. This method is often called 'pseudo-time', as the distance is based on the two different measurements where the difference in depth is taken from the CPT logging. First, a depth measurement d1 is performed, after which the probe is pressed down to the depth d2 to continue with the next measurement, Figure \ref{fig:08} (b).

An alternative to the above is to combine the limestone probe with seismic CPT approach. In cases where a more reliable determination of the properties is needed, we recommend the application of these alternatives. An alternative to using seismic CPT is the up hole technique for quality control of the LC columns. This methodology involves installing a pipe in or near the centre of the column. The technology was developed for installation of the measuring pipes in connection with the investigations for SGI SD Technical Report 35, which means that the pipe is installed with the help of the LC column machine. This can be a good solution to quickly and easily install the pipes for drilling. 

Upon the initial hardening, the measurements can begin. On the top of the LC pillar, for example, four accelerometers are installed, positioned with a 90 \textdegree angle between the accelerometers. The measurement consists of the P- and S-wave sources being lowered into the pipe to the desired depth, after which the probe is clamped as a "packer" and the waves are excited and the walking speed is measured, Figure \ref{fig:09}. The experiment is repeated in the directions where the accelerometers are placed. After the measurements are finished on one level, the probe is moved up to the next level, after which the measurements continue in an iterative manner.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_09.jpg}
	\caption{Schematic sketch of the 'up hole' measurements of a LC column.}
\label{fig:09}
\end{figure}

The advantage of this method is that it ensures a good contact between the columns and the accelerometers. Besides, the excitation wave and the location of the seismic source are very well defined. An additional advantage is that the measurements can be carried out by one person without costs for drilling rigs, which significantly decreases the financial costs in the project. However, the identified disadvantages of this method are the following. The quality of the columns is usually the worst at the top. This can be solved either by excavating the top or by attaching the accelerometers at a deeper level. A disadvantage compared to the seismic CPT is that it is necessary to decide in advance which columns are to be measured. Therefore, in order to obtain a direct comparison with the limestone probe, a slotted pipe can be used for the up hole measurements, after which a normal probing with the limestone probe is carried out.

\subsection{Shear strength evaluation from CPT}

The evaluation of the CPT probe has been performed using Conrad software, version 3.1. In a fine-grained soil, shear strength is primarily evaluated as the net peak pressure. An alternative method for clays is to use the generated pore overpressure. The empirical relationships developed for the conditions of Swedish soil are based on the evaluated dataset containing field measurements and laboratory data from a variety of soil types collected in Sweden. For natural clay soil, the relationship between the net peak pressure and shear strength is sensitive to the yield strength of soil (\emph{wL}) and to some extent also to the degree of the over-consolidation (OCR), which is described according to the Equation \ref{eq:03}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:03}
	c_u = \frac{q_t -\sigma _{\nu 0}}{13,4+6,65W_L} {\left(\frac{OCR}{1,3}\right)}^{-0,2}
\end{equation}

In cases where a measure of the yield strength of clay is missing, the Equation \ref{eq:04} is used instead: 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:04}
	c_u \approx \frac{q_t -\sigma _{\nu 0}}{N_{kt}}
\end{equation}

Where $N_{kt}$ (empirical confactor) is set to 16.3 for clay. In the Conrad evaluation software, $N_{kt}$ is denoted by $N_{11}$. For a binder-stabilized soil, there is not the same amount of the empirical evidence and therefore, not much literature about which value of $N_{kt}$ should be used. There are recommendations that $N_{kt}$ values should be set between 17--20 for a stabilized soil \citep{Larsson2017,Makusa}.

Furthermore, there are limitations with evaluating the firmness in different layers of highly layered soil, because the tip pressure is affected from the above as underlying layer at a distance of 5 to 20 tip diameters. To evaluate a firmer layer in a loose soil using this method, the thickness of the layer must be between 4.4--0.7 m. In the opposite case, with a looser layer in a firmer soil, the thickness of soil layer should be between 0.2 to 0.4 m. This means that the zones of weakness in a LC column can easily be missed in a CPT evaluation. This becomes the most important issue for the evaluation of a LC column reinforcement in the shear zone.

In a binder-stabilized soil, the pozzolanic reactions take place and continue for many months, which is why the properties of a stabilized soil (texture, structure, strength, etc) partly change over time. The change in the texture of clay is based on the exchange of ions in the clay and change in water ratio. The ion exchange is described by the lyotropic series, as below: 

\ch{Li+} $<$ \ch{Na+} $<$ \ch{H+} $<$ \ch{K+} $<$ \ch{NH4+} $<$$<$ \ce{Mg^{++}} $<$ \ch{Ca^{++}} $<$$<$ \ch{Al^{+++}}.

The change in the structure of the clay mineral is shown in Figure \ref{fig:10}.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_10.jpg}
	\caption{Change in texture and water-holding of clay as it moves from a sodium saturated system to a calcium-saturated system}
\label{fig:10}
\end{figure}

This means that the stabilized clay has change in plasticity, yield strength, and other parameters. The degree of change depends both on the type of clay mineral and on the type of binder. The changes to the structure of the stabilized clay mean that the empirical relationships that exist between the tip pressure and shear strength of the original non-stabilised clay differ for a stabilized clay. To evaluate samples cured at different temperatures, soil specimens can be compared, e.g., laboratory-processed samples cured in climate rooms at 7\textdegree C and samples fabricated \emph{in situ} under different temperature conditions. The maturity number is defined according to Equation \ref{eq:05}: 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:05}
	M_T =[ 20+(T-20)\times K]^2 \times \sqrt{t}
\end{equation}

Where T = temperature (\textdegree C), t = time (days), K = material-dependent empirical constant.
The material-dependent empirical constant K is calculated by curing samples at different temperatures, e.g., 7\textdegree C and 20\textdegree C. The samples were then measured with resonance frequency measurement at different time intervals. The results from the seismic measurements were used to fit the curves so that they coincide, i.e., different values of K are used, after which the curves are compared. A common value of K accepted in this study is 0.5, although the literature has also shown $K \approx 1$ \cite{Ahnberg}.

\subsection{Results of the L/C pillar recipes}
\subsubsection{Strength determination}

In the testing of binder combinations, a reference recipe of OPC CEM II and QL were used in a mutual ratio of 50/50\%. The evaluated shear strength for reference samples at different curing times is reported in Figure \ref{fig:11}. Here the quantities of binders represented 80, 100 and 120 kg of binders per $m^3$ of clay. The samples were stored in a climate room with a temperature of 7\textdegree C. The results show the higher strength in the samples stabilized with 120 kg of binders per $m^3$,  of clay, while the lowest values for the 80 kg of binder. Thus, on day 80 of stabilization, the highest values of shear strength reached 430, 45 and 465 kPa for soil mixtures stabilized with 80, 100 and 120 kg of binders, respectively. This indicates taht the amount of binders directly affects the strength of soil in the final output. 

\begin{figure}[H]
	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{Fig_11.jpg}
	\caption{Shear strength as a function of storage time for test specimens stabilized by a combination of CEM II/A and burned lime (50/50).}
\label{fig:11}
\end{figure}

In order to evaluate the effects of different binder combinations on shear strength of the stabilized soil material, a test setup according to the simplex centroid was used supported by Pareto diagrams, Figure \ref{fig:12}. The Pareto chart shows the effect of the different components of binders on the stabilization results with achieved significance value \emph{p}=0.05. For a binder amount at $80 kg/m^3$, the results show a clear positive interaction between various components (OPC, QL and LKD) in a binder combination, Figure \ref{fig:12}.

Direct and positive interaction means that binder components generate a higher strength than can be expected from a linear regression between the effects of the individual binders. The model has an explanation rate of 95\%, i.e., it explains 95\% of the variations in shear strength. The setup of test for binder amount of $100 kg/m^3$ clay contains an extended number of tests. For comparison with other amounts of binders, the analysis was performed with the same number of trial points as for 80 and 120 kg of binders, respectively. The result from this analysis shows a lower positive interaction between the binders. However, this should be compared with the result from the analysis of the extended trials. In a trial setup with more internal trial points, the resolution increases and the degree of the explanation rate of model increases from 95\% to 97.5\%. The extended model shows a greater interaction between various binder components for mix amount corresponding to 120 kg of binder per $m^3$ of clay. At this mix amount, the positive interaction of the binder components was rather small, i.e., insignificant. The degree of explanation of the response surface, according to Response Surface Methodology (RSM) \cite{MyersMontgomery,Montgomery} was 98\%.

\subsubsection{Seismic measurements}

Seismic measurements were carried out as the P-wave measurements, according to the resonance frequency method. The P-wave is an axial wave passing through the cylindrical specimen. In this case, the P-wave was correlated against the shear strength of the stabilized material. For the dimensioning of LC columns, the values of shear strength of the material were used. 

Thus, various binder recipes were assessed, according to the shear strength of soil specimens obtained after a certain curing time. The connection between the P-wave velocity and the compressive strength is indirect and material-dependent, i.e., it depends on the composition of soil material. A correlation between the S-wave velocity and shear strength of the stabilized soil is reported in Figure \ref{fig:13}(a). Here, shear wave velocity is adjusted according to the Equations \ref{eq:06} and \ref{eq:07}, \cite{VERASTEGUIFLORES2015943}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:06}
	\tau _{fu}=0,0424\times {V_s}^{1,462}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:07}
	\tau _{fu}=0,0021\times {V_s}^{1,9244}
\end{equation}

The results from the above measurements can be used for shear strength prediction based on measured P- and S-wave velocity from the \emph{in situ} measurements, e.g., with down hole measurements. For Norvik project, the major measurements were the P-waves. The correlation between the P-wave and shear strength is reported in Figure \ref{fig:13}(b).

\section{Conclusion}

The laboratory testing showed a high repeatability between the trials of seismic measurements. The variation in values of compressive strength and shear strength increased slightly along with the period of stabilization and increased strength of soil, which is expected, as minor defects and inaccuracies in the test specimens have a greater effect on strength and result in higher resistance. 

The results from the field test show large variations in tip resistance, but also large variations in shear and compression wave velocities. These results were partly expected but the hypothesis was to find the same trends in the peak pressure and in seismic results. At the seismic CPT there are several uncertainty factors regarding seismic measurements which will be investigated in further steps of research:

\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{-0.1cm}
	\item How are the generated S and P waves transmitted to the column? 
	\item Is there cross signals originating from other sources of vibration?
	\item Is the probe in or coming out of the column?
	\item How well shear and compression waves are generated at greater depths? 
\end{itemize}
    
Seismic CPT has many advantages over the traditional CPT. To ensure that the CPT probe is in the column, a resistivity CPT can be connected there because the stabilized soil has a much lower resistivity due to free ions \cite{Dahlinetal1999}. One of the great advantages of seismic CPT is that one obtains both a measure of the compressive force and a determination of the P- and S-waves. 

Another important parameter for seismic CPT is the financial costs of the works and technical equipment. Here, both a drilling rig and special expensive equipment are required, as well as the fact that the method is currently quite time-consuming and financially costly. By using a better signal source in seismic methods, some of the disadvantages of the methods can be eliminated. Therefore, this methodology can be a way to evaluate the effects of LC columns but cannot compete in terms of cost with the limestone column probes.
